# v2.6: present and differentials, junction and rescue lines, management ladder.
# Same syntax as fw1. No em dashes.

@@ present | 🎤 | Present to the examiner and give differentials | Conclusion first, then the evidence for and against, then three reasoned tiers of differential, then what you would do. The same seven moves after a history, an examination or a data station.
step: head | !Headline: who, what you did, your one-line impression | Mr Jones is a 61-year-old man who presented with ___. My findings are consistent with ___. | The examiner decides in your first sentence whether you know what is going on. Lead with the conclusion; never narrate "first I looked at the hands, then…".
step: pos | !Key positives: the two or three findings that support it | The key findings were ___, ___ and ___. | Group findings by what they mean, not the order you found them. Three is enough; a list of ten hides the important one.
step: neg | !Important negatives: the absent signs that rule out the dangerous lookalike or a complication | Importantly, there was no ___, which makes ___ less likely. | A negative only scores when you say what it excludes. The "which makes" clause is your reasoning made audible.
step: comp | To complete: three named items | To complete my assessment, I would like to ___, ___ and ___. | A named mark in every examination scheme. Pick items that would change the diagnosis: observations, a urine dip, the joint above and below, a full neurological examination.
step: ddx | !Differentials in three tiers, each with a because | The most likely diagnosis is ___ because ___. A common alternative is ___, which I would tell apart by ___. The important one not to miss is ___, so I would ___. | Three reasoned tiers beat a list of seven. Likely, lookalike, must-not-miss is how seniors think, and each tier carries its reason.
step: ix | Investigations, in the order you would do them | I would start with bedside tests: ___. Then bloods: ___. Then imaging: ___, and if needed ___. | Bedside → bloods → imaging → special. An ordered list sounds safe; a random one sounds like guessing.
step: mx | Management first steps, and who you would tell | Initially I would ___, and I would discuss this with my senior today. | Ending on an action with its urgency earns the management mark most students leave on the table.
nn: Turn to the examiner and present by meaning, never in the order you did things
nn: Every differential carries a "because"
nn: Only present findings you actually elicited; if unsure, say so
nn: When you have finished, stop and let the examiner ask

@ afterhx | After a history | PP05, PP06, PP22, PP55, PP107 |
use: "Please summarise and tell me what you think is going on." Thank the patient, then turn your body to the examiner.
ch: head | Mr Jones is a 61-year-old smoker with 45 minutes of central crushing chest pain radiating to his left arm, with sweating. Who, what, how long, and the one risk factor that matters.
ch: pos | History positives: the character, the associated symptoms and the risk factors that load the dice.
ch: neg | The red flags you asked about that are absent: "He has no tearing pain through to the back, no haemoptysis and no calf swelling."
ch: comp | Swap "to complete" for the examination you would do next: "I would like to examine his cardiovascular system and check his observations."
ch: ix | Bedside first: observations, ECG, urine dip, capillary glucose. Then the one specific blood test.
tree: Most likely | ACS, because of central crushing pain at rest with sweating in a smoker
tree: Common alternative | GORD, told apart by the link to meals and lying flat, and a normal ECG and troponin
tree: Must not miss | Aortic dissection: tearing pain to the back and unequal arm blood pressures mean a CT aortogram
pearl: Who, what, how long, why it matters; then likely, lookalike, killer.

@ exab | After an abnormal examination | PP09, PP14, PP34, PP84 |
use: "Present your findings." Cover the patient, thank them, then face the examiner. No notes, no list of every step.
ch: head | Today I examined Mrs Patel, a 68-year-old woman, and my findings are consistent with a right-sided pleural effusion.
ch: pos | Positives grouped by meaning: "reduced expansion on the right, stony dull percussion and reduced breath sounds and vocal resonance at the right base."
ch: neg | "Importantly, the trachea is central and there is no clubbing or lymphadenopathy, so there is no sign of mediastinal shift or an obvious malignant cause."
ch: comp | To complete, I would check her observations including oxygen saturations, review the temperature chart and request a chest X-ray.
tree: Most likely | Pleural effusion, because of stony dull percussion with reduced breath sounds and vocal resonance
tree: Common alternative | Consolidation, told apart by bronchial breathing and increased vocal resonance
tree: Must not miss | A malignant effusion: weight loss, smoking or clubbing mean a CXR, then an ultrasound-guided pleural tap with cytology
pearl: Inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation are only evidence; present the meaning.

@ exnorm | After a normal examination | PP09, PP84 |
use: You found nothing. Many examination stations use volunteers with no signs, so a confident normal presentation is common and scores.
ch: head | "This was a normal ___ examination, with no signs of ___ or ___." Name the conditions you were looking for.
ch: pos | Say the normal findings that matter for the complaint: "The pulse is regular at 72, the heart sounds are normal with no added sounds."
ch: neg | The negatives are the presentation here: list the signs you looked for that were absent.
ch: ddx | If the station gave a complaint, still give three tiers for it: a normal examination does not cancel the history.
nn: Never invent a sign to sound clever
pearl: Normal is a finding: say what you excluded.

@ data | After a data station: ECG, X-ray, gas, bloods | PP01, PP15, PP14, PP102 |
use: You are handed an ECG, a film, a gas or a set of bloods and asked to interpret it.
ch: head | "This is a 12-lead ECG of John Smith, aged 65, taken today at 10:15. The most striking abnormality is ___."
ch: pos | The abnormalities in your systematic order, the most important first.
ch: neg | The negative that would change management: no ST elevation, no free air under the diaphragm, no pneumothorax.
ch: ddx | "In summary, this shows ___, consistent with ___." Then the alternative and the must-not-miss.
ch: mx | Act on it: "Given this, I would ___ and inform my senior now."
pearl: Who and when, adequate, the striking thing, the reassuring negative, the action.

@ ddq | When the examiner asks "What are your differentials?" | |
use: Mid-station or at the end. The question is a gift: the examiner is pointing at the marks.
ch: ddx | Three, not seven: "The most likely is ___ because ___. A common alternative is ___, which I would tell apart by ___. The important one not to miss is ___." If pushed for more, sort by system or by the surgical sieve.
tree: Likely | The best fit for the story and signs, with the clue that clinches it
tree: Common alternative | The commonest lookalike, with the one detail that splits them
tree: Must not miss | The dangerous one, with the test that excludes it
pearl: Likely, lookalike, killer: each with its "because".

@ ixq | When asked "How would you investigate?" | |
ch: ix | Bedside: observations, ECG, urine dip, glucose, pregnancy test. Bloods: FBC, U&Es, LFTs, CRP and the one specific test. Imaging: first line, then definitive. Special: endoscopy, biopsy, echo, lumbar puncture.
pearl: Bedside, bloods, imaging, special: cheapest and quickest first.

@ mxq | When asked "How would you manage?" | |
ch: mx | Say the urgency first, then run the 🗺 management ladder: bedside, bloods, imaging, conservative, the named first-line drug, specialist with urgency, safety-net.
pearl: Urgency first, then the ladder.

@@ flow | 🔀 | What to say next: junction lines and rescues | Marks are lost at the joins, not in the content. One pre-learnt line for every change of section, timed for a 10-minute station, plus what to say when it goes wrong.
step: open | !Open, minute 0 | Hello, I'm Nana, a fourth-year medical student. Can I check your full name and date of birth? I've been asked to ___. Is that OK? | Every opening anchor in one breath, so your head is free to watch the patient.
step: pc | Open question, then let them talk, by 30 seconds | What's brought you in today? … Tell me more about that. | The golden minute. Actors give their planted clues early if you do not interrupt.
step: hpc | !Into the details, by about 1½ minutes | Thank you, that's really helpful. I'd like to ask some more specific questions about the ___ itself. | Signposting was flagged in your feedback. One sentence makes your structure audible.
step: rf | Into the dangerous questions, by about 3½ minutes | I'm going to ask a few questions to make sure we're not missing anything serious. | Framing red flags this way stops them sounding frightening, and tells the examiner you are hunting the killer.
step: bg | Into the background, by about 5 minutes | Now I'd like to step back and ask about your general health, medicines and day-to-day life. | Drugs, allergies, smoking, alcohol, work and driving hide the station-winners: the pill in a PE, NSAIDs in a bleed, a lorry licence after a fit.
step: ice | !Hand it back to the patient, by about 6½ minutes | Before I go on, what were your own thoughts about what might be going on? Is there anything in particular worrying you? | The actor's hidden worry usually comes out only when you ask directly.
step: sum | !Summarise, by about 7 minutes | Let me summarise to check I've got this right: ___. Have I missed anything? | Shows structure and lets the patient correct you. Also flagged in your feedback.
step: plan | !Plan or examiner, at 8 minutes | Based on what you've told me, I think the most likely cause is ___. What I'd like to do next is ___. / To the examiner: Would you like me to present my findings? | Stop gathering at 8:00 whatever is left. The plan is worth more than the last questions.
step: close | Close, by 9½ minutes | If you notice ___, please call 999 or come straight back. Is there anything you'd like to ask me? Thank you. | Specific safety-netting and an invitation for questions are scored in almost every scheme.
nn: Signpost every change of section in one sentence
nn: At 8:00 stop gathering, whatever you have not asked
nn: One question at a time; no double questions

@ blank | Your mind goes blank | |
use: You lose your place or cannot think of the next question.
ch: sum | Summarise out loud: "So far you've told me ___." It buys 20 seconds and the gap usually shows itself.
ch: bg | If the middle has gone, jump to the background: medicines, allergies, smoking and alcohol are always marks.
ch: ice | Ask ICE: the actor's worry often tells you where the station is heading.
nn: Never sit in silence: summarise, then ask "Is there anything else you think I should know?"
pearl: Lost? Summarise. The summary is the map.

@ cue | The patient drops a cue or gets upset | |
use: A throwaway line such as "my dad had something like this", a pause, tears, or a change in tone.
ch: pc | "You mentioned ___. Can you tell me a bit more about that?" Follow the cue the first time you hear it; picking up cues is a marked communication item.
ch: ice | Name the emotion before the next question: "I can see this has really worried you." Then pause and let them answer.
nn: Emotion before information
pearl: Name it, normalise it, then ask.

@ interrupt | The examiner interrupts or asks you directly | |
use: "What's your differential?", "Please move on to the plan", or a question mid-examination.
ch: plan | Stop, answer exactly what was asked in one or two sentences, then offer: "Would you like me to carry on with ___?"
nn: Never argue or say "I was getting to that"
pearl: The examiner's question tells you where the marks are.

@ dontknow | You don't know the answer | |
use: A dose, a guideline, a test you cannot remember.
ch: plan | "I'm not certain. I would check the BNF and local guidelines and discuss with my senior. What I would do now to keep the patient safe is ___."
nn: Never bluff a dose or a fact
pearl: Safe and honest beats confident and wrong.

@ forgot | You realise you missed something | |
use: Halfway through the background you remember you never asked about radiation, or about allergies.
ch: sum | "Sorry, before we move on, could I go back and ask about ___?" Going back openly still earns the mark.
pearl: Going back is a mark; leaving it out is not.

@ time | Time is running out | |
use: The one-minute warning, or you reach 8:00 still asking questions.
ch: plan | Drop the remaining questions. One-sentence summary, the most likely diagnosis with its reason, the next step.
ch: close | Always safety-net, even in one line: "If it gets worse or you notice ___, call 999."
pearl: At 8:00 the plan beats the perfect history.

@ pushback | The examiner challenges your answer | |
use: "Are you sure?" or "What else could it be?"
ch: plan | "That's a fair point. The other possibility is ___, and ___ would help tell them apart." Reconsider aloud; change your answer only if the new reasoning is better.
pearl: Reason aloud; "Are you sure?" is not a reason to flip.

@ angry | The patient or relative is angry | |
use: Raised voice, crossed arms, "Nobody has told me anything."
ch: open | Acknowledge before anything else: "I can see you're really frustrated, and I'd like to help." Sit down, open posture, let them finish.
ch: ice | Ask what they have been told and what would help most.
nn: Never be defensive; apologise for their experience without blaming colleagues
pearl: Angry people need to be heard before they can listen.

@@ plan | 🗺 | Management plan: any condition | Run the same ladder every time you reach 8:00 or the examiner asks "How would you manage?" Only what sits on each rung changes.
step: now | !Urgency: is it urgent? Say so first | This could be serious, so I would assess him with an A to E approach, start ___ and involve my senior now. | If the patient is unwell, A to E and escalation come before any list. Saying the urgency first shows you can triage.
step: bed | Bedside: observations, ECG, urine dip, glucose, pregnancy test, peak flow | At the bedside I would check his observations with a NEWS2 score and ___. | The cheapest tests are the quickest marks and catch the planted abnormal observation.
step: bl | Bloods: the core set plus the one specific test | Bloods: full blood count, U&Es, LFTs, CRP, and specifically ___. | The specific test is the mark: troponin, D-dimer, HbA1c, TFTs, lipase, tTG.
step: img | Imaging and special tests | Imaging: ___ first, then ___ if needed. | Name the first-line test, then the definitive one.
step: cons | Conservative and lifestyle | I would explain the diagnosis, give a leaflet and talk about ___. | Smoking, alcohol, weight, exercise and driving are a rung on almost every NICE ladder and cost one sentence.
step: med | !Medical: the first-line drug, named | First line would be ___. | Name the class and, if you know it, the drug. "Some medication" scores nothing.
step: spec | Specialist, surgical or referral, with its urgency | I would refer to ___ on the ___ pathway. | Urgency is the mark: 999, same day, suspected cancer pathway, routine.
step: sn | !Safety-net and follow-up | If you develop ___, call 999. I'd like to see you again in ___. | Specific warning signs plus a follow-up date close every management answer.
nn: Urgency before detail
nn: Name the drug class, never just "medication"
nn: Say who you would tell, and how fast

@ chest | Chest pain, ACS, hypertension and PE | PP06, PP05, PP13, PP20 | 0, 1, 2, 4, 13
ch: now | Suspected ACS: aspirin 300 mg, GTN, oxygen only if saturations are below 94%, an opioid with an antiemetic if pain persists, ECG and senior now; STEMI goes for primary PCI. Suspected dissection: no antiplatelets, urgent CT aortogram.
ch: bed | ECG within 10 minutes and repeated; observations; blood pressure in both arms if dissection is possible.
ch: bl | High-sensitivity troponin at presentation and repeated per local protocol; FBC, U&Es, glucose, lipids. D-dimer only if the Wells score says PE is unlikely.
ch: img | CXR. CTPA if Wells says PE is likely. CT aortogram for dissection. CT coronary angiography for new stable chest pain. Echo after an MI.
ch: cons | Stop smoking, cardiac rehabilitation after an MI, diet and exercise, check DVLA rules before driving.
ch: med | Stable angina: GTN spray plus a beta-blocker or a calcium channel blocker, with aspirin and a statin. After an MI: dual antiplatelets, ACE inhibitor, beta-blocker, atorvastatin 80 mg. Hypertension: ACE inhibitor or ARB if under 55 or with type 2 diabetes; calcium channel blocker if 55 or over, or of Black African or African-Caribbean family origin without diabetes.
ch: spec | STEMI: primary PCI. NSTEMI: cardiology for angiography guided by the GRACE score. Type A dissection: cardiothoracic surgery.
ch: sn | Angina: if the pain has not gone 5 minutes after the second GTN dose, call 999.
pearl: Aspirin and GTN for all; oxygen only if hypoxic; morphine only if needed.

@ sob | Breathlessness: asthma, COPD and heart failure | PP18, PP17, PP10, PP101 | 6, 15, 16, 12

@ mh | Mental health | PP65, PP66, PP62, PP63 | 100, 102, 103, 110, 111
ch: now | Risk first. Immediate risk to self or others: do not leave them alone; crisis team or psychiatric liaison today. Paracetamol overdose: level at 4 hours, acetylcysteine by the treatment line. Alcohol withdrawal: chlordiazepoxide and IV thiamine as Pabrinex.
ch: bed | Mental state examination, risk assessment, AUDIT for alcohol, observations.
ch: bl | Organic screen: FBC, U&Es, LFTs, TFTs, glucose, calcium, B12; urine drug screen. Before lithium: U&Es and eGFR, TFTs, calcium, weight, ECG if cardiac risk.
ch: cons | Psychological: guided self-help or group CBT for less severe depression; CBT plus an antidepressant for more severe. Social: housing, money, work, support. Psychosis: CBT for psychosis and family intervention.
ch: med | Depression: an SSRI such as sertraline, reviewed at 2 weeks, or at 1 week if under 25 or at higher risk. Lithium: levels 12 hours after the dose, usually 0.6 to 0.8 mmol/L. Psychosis: an oral antipsychotic chosen with the patient.
ch: spec | First-episode psychosis: early intervention in psychosis service within 2 weeks.
ch: sn | A crisis plan: who to call, NHS 111 option 2 for urgent mental health help, Samaritans on 116 123, 999 if in danger.
pearl: Bio, psycho, social, plus a safety plan: four rungs for every psychiatry plan.

@ ent | ENT | PP79, PP81, PP82, PP60, PP80 | 140, 144, 149, 150, 151
ch: now | Epistaxis: sit forward, pinch the soft part of the nose for 10 to 15 minutes; then cautery or packing. Drooling, stridor or a muffled voice: senior anaesthetist and ENT, do not examine the throat.
ch: bed | Otoscopy, tuning fork tests, FeverPAIN or Centor score, observations.
ch: bl | Monospot if glandular fever is suspected; FBC and clotting for heavy or recurrent epistaxis.
ch: img | MRI of the internal auditory meatus for unilateral sensorineural hearing loss or tinnitus.
ch: cons | Sore throat: fluids, paracetamol or ibuprofen; most settle within a week. Sinusitis: saline irrigation; most is viral.
ch: med | Sore throat with FeverPAIN 4 to 5: phenoxymethylpenicillin for 5 to 10 days, or a back-up prescription; 2 to 3: consider no or back-up antibiotics. Otitis externa: antibiotic drops with or without a steroid. Sinusitis over 10 days: high-dose nasal steroid for 14 days.
ch: spec | Neck lump or persistent hoarseness at 45 or over: suspected cancer pathway. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss: same-day ENT.
ch: sn | Difficulty swallowing saliva, noisy breathing or a muffled voice with a sore throat: call 999.
pearl: One-sided sensorineural loss means an MRI until proven otherwise.

@ eye | Eyes | PP75, PP77, PP78, PP76 | 154, 158, 160, 162, 163
ch: now | Acute angle closure: same-day ophthalmology, lie flat, pilocarpine, acetazolamide, analgesia and an antiemetic. Retinal detachment, retinal artery occlusion, or GCA with visual loss: same day.
ch: bed | Visual acuity first, every time; pupils for an RAPD; fields; fundoscopy; fluorescein for a corneal ulcer.
ch: bl | ESR and CRP if over 50 with visual loss and headache.
ch: img | Slit lamp and optical coherence tomography in eye casualty.
ch: cons | Contact lens wearers: stop wearing lenses. Driving: check the DVLA visual standard.
ch: med | Bacterial conjunctivitis: usually self-limiting; chloramphenicol drops if severe. Open-angle glaucoma: a prostaglandin analogue such as latanoprost. Wet AMD: anti-VEGF injections.
ch: spec | Red eye with pain, photophobia or reduced vision: same-day eye casualty. Suspected wet AMD: macular service within 1 week.
ch: sn | Sudden loss of vision, a curtain over the vision, or new flashes and floaters: same day.
pearl: Visual acuity is the vital sign of the eye: document it first.
